2. Related Work


2.1 Measurement and Psychometric Foundations of Student Burnout

The Maslach Burnout Inventory, Student Survey (MBI-SS) remains the most widely adopted instrument for assessing burnout in student populations, a finding confirmed by a systematic review of 38 studies spanning primary, secondary, and university levels [P008]. Adaptations of the MBI-SS have been validated in diverse cultural settings, including Colombia [P007], Thailand [P013], and Hungary [P033]. In Colombia, a 14-item model (excluding one item) demonstrated adequate goodness-of-fit and high internal consistency [P007], while a Thai translation similarly produced acceptable factor structures [P013]. A Hungarian study validated a University Demand-Resource Questionnaire (UDRQ) alongside the MBI-SS, showing that the Job Demand-Resource (JD-R) framework could explain substantial proportions of variance in exhaustion, cynicism, and reduced accomplishment [P033].

Despite its dominance, the MBI-SS has faced growing criticism. A systematic review applying the COSMIN psychometric quality guidelines found that no existing student burnout instrument currently meets the criteria for recommendation, primarily because of inadequate content validity [P027]. The reviewed instruments, including the MBI-SS and newer alternatives like the Burnout Assessment Tool (BAT-C), were originally designed for occupational settings and subsequently adapted for students, often without rigorous qualitative input from the student population itself [P027]. This finding underscores an important methodological vulnerability across the broader field. A separate study validated the BAT-C among Italian university students, establishing measurement invariance across gender and worker status, though it also relied exclusively on self-report data [P024].

Alongside traditional academic burnout, newer constructs have emerged to capture technology-specific experiences. The Digital Burnout Scale (DBS) was culturally adapted and validated for Generation Z university students in South Korea, measuring dimensions such as "digital aging" and "digital deprivation" that are absent from conventional academic burnout scales [P019]. Similarly, a Conversational AI Dependence Scale (CAIDS) was developed and validated in China to measure students' uncontrollability, withdrawal symptoms, mood modification, and negative impacts associated with generative AI tools [P014]. In the programming education domain, a scale measuring generative AI dependency was developed using an Affect-Behavior-Cognition (ABC) framework, capturing students' anxiety when AI is unavailable, habitual reliance on AI-generated code, and belief that AI output is superior to their own work [P028]. A burnout scale was also developed specifically for students in English as a Medium of Instruction (EMI) programs in Saudi Arabia, addressing the combined academic and language-related stressors unique to that population [P032]. These newer instruments reflect an evolving understanding that student burnout is not a unitary phenomenon but is shaped by the specific demands of contemporary learning environments.


2.2 Risk Factors and Predictors: Traditional Statistical Approaches

A large body of the literature relies on traditional inferential statistics, particularly regression and structural equation modeling (SEM), to identify the determinants of student burnout. These studies converge on several broad categories of predictors, though they differ in emphasis and population focus.

Emotion regulation has been consistently linked to burnout outcomes. In a South Korean sample of undergraduates, hierarchical multiple regression revealed that rumination, expressive suppression, and the absence of adaptive strategies such as cognitive reappraisal and problem-solving were significant predictors, with psychological distress mediating the relationship between maladaptive regulation and burnout [P003]. The role of emotional regulation difficulties also surfaced in a Portuguese study that incorporated lifestyle variables, where difficulties in emotion regulation were among the strongest predictors identified through a combination of unsupervised clustering and supervised machine learning [P009].

Career-related and academic stressors represent another well-documented pathway to burnout. Among Bangladeshi undergraduates, Partial Least Squares SEM demonstrated that both self-evaluation traits (internal resources) and career decision-making difficulties (situational demands) predicted burnout, with social support partially mediating these effects [P011]. In Indonesia, academic stress was a strong positive predictor of burnout among final-year thesis-writing students, while grit (perseverance) acted as a significant protective factor [P022]. A Chilean study of health sciences students found that the burnout dimensions manifested differently by gender: female students were more prone to emotional exhaustion, whereas male students and those with paid employment exhibited higher depersonalization [P025].

Sociodemographic, institutional, and lifestyle factors have also been examined extensively. Studies conducted at a large German technical university identified study satisfaction as a robust protective factor against exhaustion, while academic workload modestly elevated the risk for cynicism [P018, P026]. These same studies broadened the inquiry beyond the customary focus on medical students, revealing that informatics and engineering students reported the highest burnout levels [P026]. In a Chinese Traditional Chinese Medicine university, logistic regression identified irregular daily routines, lower academic ranking, and religious affiliation as risk factors, while rural pre-university residence and perceived teacher support were protective [P016]. The association between health behaviors and burnout has also been explored: subjective fitness and perceived dietary healthiness were more strongly associated with lower burnout than the objective volume of physical activity [P034]. A narrative review of physician and medical student burnout synthesized evidence on the systemic role of electronic health record workload and technostress as burnout drivers, alongside traditional factors like time constraints and lack of support [P023].


2.3 Digital Technology, Smartphone Dependence, and AI-Related Burnout

A distinct thematic strand in the literature concerns the relationship between digital technology use and student mental health outcomes. Mobile phone addiction has been linked to academic burnout through multiple pathways. In one Chinese study, technology conflict mediated the association between phone addiction and burnout, while mindfulness moderated both the direct and indirect effects, suggesting that self-regulatory capacity can buffer the harm of excessive device use [P017]. A separate study identified learning engagement as a mediator in the same relationship and found that physical exercise moderated the effect: high levels of exercise effectively neutralized the negative impact of phone addiction on engagement [P030]. In a substantially larger Chinese sample (N=23,652), smartphone dependence predicted poor sleep quality, with negative emotions and health-promoting behaviors serving as chain mediators [P020].

These findings are complemented by emerging work on AI-specific behavioral patterns. A study using PLS-SEM among Chinese college students examined the progression from perceived usefulness and inert thinking through AI dependence and AI addiction to learning burnout, applying the I-PACE addiction framework to generative AI tools [P012]. Another study in Saudi Arabia found that AI application usage patterns explained a meaningful proportion of variance in graduate student burnout, although the direction of the effect was nuanced: higher proficiency with AI tools was associated with lower, not higher, burnout, suggesting that AI use may function as a protective resource when paired with adequate skills [P015]. These two studies offer contrasting perspectives, with one highlighting pathological AI dependence as a burnout risk [P012] and the other framing skilled AI use as a buffer against it [P015].

A systematic review confirmed that the broader field has recognized problematic internet use and excessive digital technology exposure as risk factors for student burnout, though it noted that these variables were uniformly measured through self-report rather than through objective behavioral monitoring [P008]. In Indonesia, exposure to different types of religious content on social media was differentially associated with anxiety and burnout: motivational content predicted lower anxiety, while fear-based and punishment-oriented content predicted higher anxiety, and longer daily social media usage predicted higher burnout [P035]. These findings suggest that the specific nature of digital content, not merely the quantity of screen time, matters for student wellbeing.


2.4 Machine Learning and Predictive Modeling Approaches

A smaller but growing subset of the literature has moved beyond traditional statistics to employ machine learning (ML) algorithms for predicting student burnout and related mental health outcomes. These studies vary considerably in the sophistication of their pipelines, the data sources they draw upon, and the performance metrics they achieve.

Several studies have compared multiple supervised classifiers on self-reported survey data. In a Chinese adolescent sample of 4,870 participants, Support Vector Machine with an RBF kernel achieved the best performance for predicting academic burnout (AUC = 0.830, F1 = 0.750), outperforming logistic regression, naive Bayes, random forest, and boosting methods [P002]. In Nepal, a rigorous evaluation of nine ensemble and boosting algorithms found that Gradient Boosting achieved the highest average performance for multiclass student stress prediction (Accuracy = 89.55%, ROC-AUC = 98.59%), with XGBoost and LightGBM performing comparably [P005]. A study in Mexico similarly compared six classifiers, with Random Forest achieving an accuracy of 0.88 for student stress detection [P029]. In Portugal, XGBoost achieved 97.53% accuracy for classifying burnout risk profiles that had been established through K-means clustering of psychological and lifestyle variables [P009]. Across Southeast Asia, a large multi-national study (N=15,366) found that Random Forest performed best for classifying negative mental well-being among university students (AUC = 0.966), and identified a concise set of easily measurable behavioral features (BMI, sports, GPA, sedentary hours, age) as the strongest predictors [P010].

One study explored a fundamentally different data paradigm. Rather than relying on self-reported survey responses as predictors, a study of Malaysian computing undergraduates trained ML models exclusively on passively collected university administrative records (GPA, credit hours, financial assistance status) to detect burnout [P021]. Logistic Regression performed best for detecting the exhaustion dimension (F1 = 68.4%, Recall = 81.2%), but the overall predictive performance was modest, illustrating that basic administrative records alone lack the psychological depth necessary to capture burnout accurately [P021].

The most technically advanced predictive framework in the reviewed literature is EMBRACE, a multitask deep learning system combining convolutional neural networks (CNN) and long short-term memory (LSTM) networks with Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML) for few-shot domain adaptation [P006]. EMBRACE processed continuous wearable physiological data (heart rate variability, skin conductance, skin temperature, accelerometry) collected from resident physicians and achieved 94.7% accuracy for burnout prediction [P006]. It also incorporated automated end-of-day email alerts with personalized SHAP-based visualizations of daily workload and burnout predictions, representing the only system in the reviewed literature that generates individualized, actionable feedback [P006]. However, the primary clinical sample was very small (n=28), and the data collection window was limited to two to seven days per participant [P006].

In Bangladesh, an ML/XAI study applied eight classifiers to predict depression, anxiety, and stress among university students using the DASS-21 [P031]. SVM performed most stably for depression and stress, while XGBoost was selected for anxiety prediction [P031]. The study demonstrated acute methodological awareness, cautioning that its models largely relied on overlapping symptomatology (using symptoms to predict other symptoms) rather than on independent antecedent risk factors, and explicitly recommended against deploying the models as clinical screening tools [P031].


2.5 Explainable AI and Interpretability

Among the studies that employ machine learning, the adoption of Explainable AI (XAI) techniques varies. The most commonly used method is SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), which was applied in multiple studies to extract global feature importance rankings and examine the directional effects of predictors [P002, P005, P006, P029, P031]. In one study, Kernel SHAP was combined with Recursive Feature Elimination and Gaussian Graphical Model network analysis to understand not only which features predicted adolescent burnout but also how those features interrelated structurally [P002]. LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) was used alongside SHAP in two studies to provide localized, instance-level prediction transparency [P005, P031]. One study also employed Sankey diagrams to visualize the associations between features and predicted mental health outcomes [P031].

A critical methodological contribution came from a study that introduced a "residualization protocol" to audit SHAP results for construct overlap [P004]. By demonstrating that apparent cross-population stability in XAI feature hierarchies was largely a psychometric artefact of correlated predictor variables (particularly trait anxiety and burnout), this work challenged a fundamental assumption underlying many XAI interpretations in the mental health domain [P004]. The study also reported that conformal prediction intervals averaged 35.4 units on a 100-point burnout scale, suggesting that individual-level clinical prediction remains unreliable with current feature sets [P004]. This finding raises important concerns about the premature deployment of ML-based burnout screening tools.

Several ML studies did not employ formal XAI methods at all. Feature importance was assessed through less granular approaches such as variable importance measures (Mean Decrease in Accuracy, Mean Decrease in Gini Index) [P010] or the Boruta algorithm [P021, P031]. The absence of transparent interpretability tools was also noted in studies that achieved high predictive accuracy but provided limited insight into why individual predictions were made [P009].


2.6 Geographical and Contextual Gaps

The reviewed studies span a broad geographical range, including Western Europe (Germany [P018, P026, P034], Switzerland [P004], Italy [P024], Hungary [P033], Croatia [P036]), East Asia (China [P002, P012, P014, P016, P017, P020, P028, P030], South Korea [P019], Thailand [P013]), Southeast Asia (Malaysia [P021], Indonesia [P022, P035]), South Asia (Nepal [P005], Bangladesh [P011, P031]), the Middle East (Saudi Arabia [P015, P032]), Latin America (Colombia [P007], Chile [P025], Mexico [P029]), and multi-national contexts (the United States and Europe [P001], Southeast Asia [P010], global systematic reviews [P008, P023, P027]). Despite this breadth, several gaps persist. The majority of studies employing predictive ML and XAI were conducted in high-income or upper-middle-income countries, with only a few targeting lower-middle-income country (LMIC) contexts [P005, P011, P031]. Of these, only one conducted an ML/XAI study specifically in Bangladesh [P031], while another used traditional SEM to investigate burnout among Bangladeshi private university students [P011]. Multiple papers across the corpus explicitly identified the absence of research in LMICs, including Bangladesh, as an unaddressed gap [P001, P002, P004, P007, P009, P013, P015, P017, P018, P026].


2.7 Cross-Cutting Limitations and Unaddressed Research Gaps

Several limitations recur with striking consistency across the reviewed literature. The most pervasive is the near-universal reliance on cross-sectional study designs, which prevents the establishment of causal or temporal relationships between predictors and burnout outcomes [P002, P003, P004, P007, P009, P011, P012, P013, P014, P015, P016, P017, P018, P019, P020, P022, P024, P025, P026, P028, P030, P033, P034, P035, P036]. This limitation is particularly consequential for studies proposing sequential mediation pathways (e.g., the progression from AI dependence through AI addiction to burnout [P012]) or moderation models (e.g., the buffering role of mindfulness against mobile phone addiction [P017]), where the temporal ordering of variables cannot be verified.

A second major limitation is the complete reliance on self-report questionnaires for data collection. Across the corpus, the overwhelming majority of studies captured all predictor and outcome variables through subjective survey instruments [P001, P002, P003, P007, P008, P009, P011, P012, P013, P014, P015, P016, P017, P018, P019, P020, P022, P024, P025, P026, P028, P030, P031, P032, P033, P034, P035, P036]. This is problematic for constructs such as mobile phone addiction [P017, P030], screen time [P019, P020], physical activity [P034], and sleep quality [P020], where the discrepancy between self-reported and objectively measured behavior is well documented. The sole exception is EMBRACE, which utilized continuous wearable physiological data from an Empatica E4 wristband, but it was limited to a clinical sample of 28 resident physicians in a single US hospital [P006]. A systematic review explicitly highlighted the field's total reliance on self-report as a key methodological gap and called for the integration of passive digital logging and physiological sensors [P008]. Multiple individual studies echoed this call [P010, P019, P022, P028, P034].

The application of machine learning to student burnout prediction also remains limited in scope. The majority of the reviewed studies used no ML algorithms at all, relying instead on regression, SEM, or psychometric validation [P001, P003, P007, P008, P011, P012, P013, P014, P015, P016, P017, P018, P019, P020, P022, P023, P024, P025, P026, P027, P028, P030, P032, P033, P034, P035, P036]. Among the studies that did employ ML, most trained models on the same type of self-reported survey data used by traditional studies [P002, P005, P009, P010, P029, P031], raising questions about whether ML provides genuine added value over established statistical methods when the underlying data modality remains unchanged. Only two studies explored alternative data sources: one using administrative records [P021] and one using wearable physiological sensors [P006]. The integration of passive digital behavioral data, such as learning management system interaction logs, smartphone usage patterns, or keystroke dynamics, remains entirely absent from the empirical studies in this corpus. Furthermore, the use of formal XAI techniques is limited to a handful of studies [P002, P004, P005, P006, P029, P031], and no study has yet combined passive digital logging with explainable ML in a student burnout context. This gap represents a significant opportunity for research that can move beyond static, self-reported snapshots toward dynamic, transparent, and objectively grounded prediction of student burnout.
