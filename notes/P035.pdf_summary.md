# File Directory: papers/P035.pdf

## 1. Metadata
* **Authors & Year:** Aguswan Khotibul Umam, Mochammad Irfan Achfandhy, Alfiyana Yuliasari, Ririn Jamiah (2026)
* **Publication Venue:** Islamic Guidance and Counseling Journal
* **Target Population & Region:** University students at Islamic Higher Education Institutions (PTKIs) | Lampung, Indonesia
* **Sample Size ($N$):** 1,098

## 2. Core Focus & Psychological Metrics
* **Primary Objective:** To investigate the associations between exposure to distinct categories of religious content on social media and levels of anxiety and academic burnout among university students.
* **Burnout / Mental Health Assessment Tool:** Maslach Burnout Inventory–Student Survey (MBI-SS) for burnout, and the Social Anxiety Scale for Adolescents (SAS-A) for anxiety.

## 3. Indicators & Feature Sets
* **Digital Wellbeing / Passive Indicators:** None used.
* **Self-Reported / Survey Indicators:** A custom "Religious Content Exposure Questionnaire" assessing exposure to five types of content: compassion–tranquility, educational, inspirational–motivational, fear–punishment, and ritual–worship. Demographic/usage variables included age, institution type, primary social media platform (TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, Facebook), frequency of use, and daily duration.

## 4. Machine Learning & Explainability (XAI)
* **ML Algorithms Evaluated:** None. The study relied on traditional inferential statistics, specifically hierarchical multiple regression testing for linear, quadratic, and interaction effects via SPSS.
* **Best Performing Model & Metrics:** The hierarchical regression models explained a modest amount of variance for both Anxiety (R² = 0.037) and Burnout (R² = 0.079). Notably, motivational content predicted lower anxiety, while fear/punishment content predicted higher anxiety. For burnout, longer daily social media duration was a positive predictor, and complex curvilinear patterns emerged regarding compassion and fear content.
* **XAI / Interpretability Techniques Used:** None / Not Applicable. (Interpretability relies on standard white-box regression coefficients).

## 5. Recommendations & Interventions
* **Personalized Recommendations Provided?** No algorithmic personalized recommendations were generated.
* **Actionable System Features:** Not an actionable technological system. The study recommends non-technical interventions for universities: strengthening digital literacy education and curating/promoting motivational, skills-oriented religious content while limiting fear-based framing to foster emotional resilience among students.

## 6. Critical Evaluation & Research Gaps
* **Main Strengths:** Highly novel approach that differentiates between specific types of religious content consumed on social media, rather than treating "religiosity" as a single, monolithic protective factor; large sample size (N=1,098); captures the nuances of algorithmically curated content in an LMIC context (Indonesia).
* **Key Limitations:** The cross-sectional design precludes establishing causal direction (e.g., do anxious students seek out fear-based content, or does the content cause anxiety?); self-report measures for content consumption are highly susceptible to recall bias.
* **Unaddressed Research Gaps:** Complete reliance on subjective self-reporting for digital behavior. The study highlights the need to understand algorithmic curation but fails to use objective passive digital logging (e.g., tracking actual screen time, video watch-time, or app usage patterns) to verify content exposure; complete absence of predictive Machine Learning (ML) models or Explainable AI (XAI) frameworks; geographically limited to Indonesia, leaving gaps regarding other LMICs like Bangladesh.
