# File Directory: papers/P034.pdf

## 1. Metadata
* **Authors & Year:** Nils Olson, Renate Oberhoffer, Barbara Reiner, Thorsten Schulz (2025)
* **Publication Venue:** Societies (MDPI)
* **Target Population & Region:** University students | Germany (Technical University of Munich)
* **Sample Size ($N$):** 1,955

## 2. Core Focus & Psychological Metrics
* **Primary Objective:** To examine the associations between university students' health behaviors (e.g., diet, physical activity, substance use) and their levels of perceived stress, burnout, and study engagement, positioning healthy lifestyle habits as potential protective resources.
* **Burnout / Mental Health Assessment Tool:** Maslach Burnout Inventory Short Form for Students (MBI-SS).

## 3. Indicators & Feature Sets
* **Digital Wellbeing / Passive Indicators:** None used.
* **Self-Reported / Survey Indicators:** Psychological metrics: Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10), Utrecht Work Engagement Scale for Students (UWES-S 9), and MBI-SS. Behavioral metrics: Physical activity/sedentary behavior (IPAQ), alcohol consumption (AUDIT-C), BMI, smoking status, perceived subjective fitness, and perceived healthiness of diet.

## 4. Machine Learning & Explainability (XAI)
* **ML Algorithms Evaluated:** None. The study relied on traditional inferential statistics, specifically Pearson's correlations, Student's t-tests, ANOVA (with Welch-ANOVA and GT2 Hochberg's post hoc), and Chi-square tests.
* **Best Performing Model & Metrics:** The correlational analysis revealed that self-perceived subjective fitness and perceived healthiness of diet had the strongest negative associations with stress and burnout, and positive associations with study engagement. Interestingly, objective volume of physical activity (measured by IPAQ METs) and daily sitting hours played a comparatively minor role with very small effect sizes.
* **XAI / Interpretability Techniques Used:** None / Not Applicable. (Interpretability relies on standard white-box statistical test effect sizes like Cohen's *d*).

## 5. Recommendations & Interventions
* **Personalized Recommendations Provided?** No algorithmic personalized recommendations were provided.
* **Actionable System Features:** Not an actionable technological system. The authors recommend that universities implement holistic behavioral prevention programs. Interventions should focus heavily on improving students' health literacy (specifically regarding diet and fitness) to bolster their psychological resilience against academic stress, moving beyond purely structural or academic interventions.

## 6. Critical Evaluation & Research Gaps
* **Main Strengths:** Utilizes a very large sample size; incorporates positive psychological frameworks (study engagement) alongside negative ones (burnout); systematically assesses a broad range of lifestyle factors using established tools (IPAQ, AUDIT-C).
* **Key Limitations:** Cross-sectional design prevents the establishment of causal relationships; relies entirely on students' subjective self-assessments of their fitness and dietary health, which the authors acknowledge may be heavily biased by underlying traits like self-esteem or depression.
* **Unaddressed Research Gaps:** Complete reliance on self-report questionnaires for behavioral tracking. The authors explicitly identify the need for "more objective data on the physical fitness of students" (e.g., utilizing passive digital logging or wearable physiological sensors rather than the IPAQ). Furthermore, there is an absence of predictive Machine Learning (ML) models or Explainable AI (XAI) frameworks; geographically, the study focuses on a high-income European nation, leaving gaps regarding LMICs such as Bangladesh.
